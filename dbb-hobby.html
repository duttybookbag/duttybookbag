J-Tube Design

Standard rocket stove with horizontal feed tube, vertical burn tunnel, and heat riser. Uses 1:2:4 ratio. Best for general purpose heating and cooking. Requires proper draft for secondary combustion.

L-Tube Design

Simplified design with direct feed into burn tunnel. Uses 1:1.5:3 ratios. Better fuel control and less smoke-back risk. Ideal for cooking applications where heat control is important.

K-Style Design

Features angled feed chute for automatic stick feeding. Self-feeding design but can jam with irregular sticks. Good for continuous burning but requires uniform fuel size.

Material Considerations

Steel: 1/4" thick minimum for combustion chamber. Fire brick: 2-4" walls. Cast refractory: Best for permanent installations. Insulation is critical - use perlite, vermiculite, or ceramic fiber.

Air Intake Guidelines

Primary air should be 25-33% of system cross-sectional area. Secondary air can be added 5-7% of system size. Too much air cools combustion, too little creates smoke.

Safety & Performance

Proper ratios ensure clean combustion with minimal smoke. Metal systems need excellent insulation and will eventually wear out. Test outdoors first before permanent installation.

Why These Ratios Work - The Science Behind Rocket Stoves

The 1:2:4 J-Tube Ratio Explained

Feed Tube (1x): This is your baseline dimension. It needs to be large enough for wood sticks to slide down easily, but small enough to prevent over-feeding. If too large, you get smoke-back into the room. If too small, constant feeding becomes annoying.

Burn Tunnel (2x): Double the feed tube length creates the perfect "mixing zone" where wood gases and air turbulently combine. This length gives enough time for primary combustion while maintaining velocity. Too short = incomplete burning. Too long = heat loss.

Heat Riser (4x): Four times the feed length creates powerful draft through the "chimney effect." Hot gases rise rapidly, pulling fresh air through the system. This height ensures secondary combustion burns all the smoke particles, giving you that clean, roaring flame.

Why L-Tube Uses 1:1.5:3 Ratios

Shorter Burn Tunnel (1.5x): L-tubes feed wood directly into the combustion zone, so they need less horizontal mixing space. The 1.5x length prevents jamming while maintaining good airflow velocity.

Reduced Riser (3x): Since L-tubes are primarily for cooking, the shorter riser keeps the flame closer to your pot while still creating enough draft. Too tall and you waste heat up the chimney instead of into your food.

K-Style Modifications for Angled Feed

Extended Feed (1.2x): The angled feed needs extra length to accommodate the slope and prevent sticks from jamming. However, this makes the feed tube less reliable with irregular wood.

Enhanced Riser (3.5x): K-style stoves often burn more continuously, so they need extra riser height to handle the increased gas flow and maintain clean combustion.

The Critical 7:1 Height-to-Width Rule

Why 7-8 Times Taller: This ratio creates the ideal "chimney effect." Too short and you don't get enough draft - your fire becomes lazy and smoky. Too tall and you pull heat away from the combustion zone before gases can fully burn.

The Physics: Rising hot air creates negative pressure (suction) at the bottom. The taller the column, the stronger the pull. But there's a sweet spot - beyond 8:1, you start losing more heat than you gain in draft.

Air Intake Mathematics

25-30% Rule: Primary air intake should be 25-30% of the combustion chamber's cross-sectional area. This provides enough oxygen for complete combustion without cooling the fire too much.

Why Not More Air?: Excess air acts like a heat sink, absorbing energy and lowering combustion temperatures. Lower temps = incomplete burning = smoke and creosote. It's better to burn hot and clean than cool and dirty.

Secondary Air (5-7%): This small amount of preheated air helps burn any remaining smoke particles in the heat riser. It's the difference between yellow flames (incomplete) and blue flames (complete combustion).

Critical Success Factors

  • Insulation is Everything: Without proper insulation around the heat riser, you'll never get secondary combustion. The gases need to stay hot (1400°F+) to burn completely.
  • Cross-Section Consistency: Keep the same internal diameter throughout the system. Any restrictions create turbulence and pressure drops.
  • Material Thickness: 1/4" steel minimum for the heat riser - thinner metal warps and fails quickly under the intense heat.
  • Draft Requirements: The system needs to "roar" like a jet engine when working properly. If it's quiet, something's wrong with the ratios or insulation.
